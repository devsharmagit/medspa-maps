## What is hyperpigmentation?

**Hyperpigmentation is a common, usually harmless condition in which patches of skin become darker than the surrounding area because of excess melanin, the pigment that gives skin its color. It can appear as small spots, larger patches, or broad discoloration, most often on sun-exposed areas like the face, hands, and chest.**

Melanin is produced by cells called melanocytes, and when they’re triggered to overproduce, by the sun, inflammation, or hormones, the extra pigment shows up as darker areas. While most hyperpigmentation is a cosmetic concern rather than a medical danger, any spot that is new, changing, itching, or bleeding should be evaluated by a dermatologist to rule out skin cancer.

## What causes hyperpigmentation?

**Hyperpigmentation has three main drivers: sun exposure, skin inflammation or injury, and hormonal changes. Certain medications and genetics can also play a role.**

- **Sun exposure:** UV light stimulates melanin as a defense mechanism, producing sun spots and worsening most other pigmentation.
- **Inflammation or injury:** acne, eczema, cuts, or even cosmetic procedures can leave dark marks as the skin heals, known as post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.
- **Hormones:** pregnancy, birth control, and hormone therapy can trigger melasma, a patchy discoloration often called the “mask of pregnancy.”
- **Medications and genetics:** some drugs increase sun sensitivity or pigmentation, and a family history can make you more prone.

## What are the types of hyperpigmentation?

**The three most common types are sun (or age) spots, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and melasma. They look different, sit at different depths in the skin, and respond differently to treatment, which is why identifying the type comes first.**

| Type | Typical cause | How it looks | Depth | Outlook |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| Sun / age spots | Cumulative UV exposure | Flat brown spots on sun-exposed skin | Surface (epidermal) | Responds well |
| Post-inflammatory (PIH) | Acne, injury, inflammation | Flat marks where a blemish healed | Surface to deeper | Fades over time; treatable |
| Melasma | Hormones plus sun | Larger, symmetric patches on cheeks, forehead, lip | Often deeper / mixed | Manageable; prone to recurrence |

Depth is the key factor. Pigment sitting near the surface is generally easier and faster to treat, while deeper pigment, common in melasma, is more stubborn and can react unpredictably to aggressive treatment.

> “Not all dark spots are the same. Sun spots, post-inflammatory marks, and melasma respond to different treatments, which is why the first step is correctly identifying the type.”

## Which medspa treatments actually work on dark spots?

**The most effective in-office options are chemical peels, laser and IPL, microneedling, and prescription-strength topicals, but the right one depends on the type of pigmentation, how deep it is, and your skin tone. Many providers combine treatments for the best result.**

### Chemical peels

Chemical peels use acids to exfoliate pigmented surface cells and speed up cell turnover, revealing brighter skin underneath. Superficial and medium-depth peels can work well for sun spots and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and gentler formulas can be used cautiously across skin tones.

### Laser treatments

Lasers target pigment with focused energy. Pigment-specific lasers (such as Q-switched and picosecond devices) can break up sun spots, while fractional resurfacing lasers improve pigment along with texture. Lasers can be highly effective but require caution, they are not usually the first choice for melasma, and they demand extra care in deeper skin tones. (Our [guide to laser skin treatments](/blog/laser-skin-treatments-explained) breaks down which device suits which concern.)

### IPL / BBL (intense pulsed light)

IPL and the more advanced BBL use broadband light to target excess pigment and redness, and can noticeably even out tone. They tend to work best on lighter skin tones with sun-related spots, and are generally not recommended for melasma or for deeper skin tones, where they can worsen pigmentation.

### Microneedling

Microneedling creates controlled micro-channels in the skin that boost cell turnover and help brightening ingredients penetrate. It’s often paired with topical actives and is considered one of the safer options across a range of skin tones, making it a useful choice for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

### Prescription-strength topicals

Ingredients like hydroquinone, tretinoin, azelaic acid, kojic acid, and vitamin C can fade pigment over time and are frequently used as a first-line treatment or alongside in-office procedures. A provider can build a regimen suited to your pigmentation type and skin tone.

## What’s the best treatment for melasma?

**Melasma is best managed with a gentle, consistent combination of strict sun protection, prescription topicals, and conservative in-office treatments, not aggressive lasers, which can actually make it worse. Melasma can be controlled, but it tends to recur, so it’s managed rather than cured.**

Because melasma is driven by hormones and sun, daily broad-spectrum SPF is the foundation of any plan. Providers often start with topicals and gentle peels, using energy-based devices cautiously and only when appropriate. Setting realistic expectations matters here: improvement is very achievable, but ongoing maintenance is part of the deal.

## Is treating hyperpigmentation safe for darker skin tones?

**Yes, but device and technique choice are critical. Deeper skin tones have more active melanin, so heat- and light-based treatments like aggressive lasers and IPL can sometimes trigger more pigment or cause burns. Gentler, melanin-safe options in experienced hands are often the smarter starting point.**

Safer approaches for richer skin tones often include microneedling, superficial chemical peels, prescription topicals, and specific laser wavelengths (such as certain Nd:YAG lasers) used at conservative settings. The single most important safeguard is choosing a provider who is experienced in treating skin of color and understands how it responds.

> “In deeper skin tones, aggressive laser treatments can actually trigger more pigment. Gentler, melanin-safe options are often the smarter starting point.”

## How long does it take for dark spots to fade?

**Most people see gradual improvement over several weeks to a few months, not overnight. Surface-level pigment tends to respond faster, while deeper pigment and melasma take longer and require patience and consistency.**

A series of treatments is usually needed rather than a single session, and results depend heavily on protecting your skin from the sun throughout. Skipping SPF is the fastest way to undo your progress.

## How to prevent hyperpigmentation from coming back

**Prevention comes down to sun protection and managing your triggers. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen is the single most effective way to keep dark spots from returning or worsening.**

- Wear broad-spectrum SPF 30+ every day and reapply, even indoors near windows and on cloudy days.
- Treat acne and skin irritation early to prevent post-inflammatory marks.
- Avoid picking or squeezing blemishes, which drives pigmentation.
- Manage hormonal triggers with your provider where relevant.
- Keep up a maintenance regimen, brightening topicals and periodic treatments, as advised.

## When to see a professional

**If over-the-counter products haven’t worked, your pigmentation is widespread or worsening, or you’re not sure what type you have, it’s worth seeing a licensed provider. Correctly identifying the pigmentation is the difference between a treatment that helps and one that makes things worse.**

A professional can diagnose the type, rule out anything concerning, and build a plan matched to your skin.
